\begin{abstract}
Somatic structural variants are called by aligning the tumour and the matched normal read sets to a
reference genome and reasoning over the resulting alignments, so the aligner decides where every
read belongs before anything decides which reads are somatic. We present \Snape, a somatic structural
variant caller that reverses that order. Both read sets are partitioned by Locally Consistent
Parsing (LCP) into cores and counted into a single two-coloured graph, in which a core carried by
the tumour reads and by none of the normal reads is somatic by construction, without a reference and
without an assembly. \Snape{} then cuts each somatic region out of the reads that carry it, as a
junction contig flanked by germline anchors, and only those contigs are aligned: on four PacBio
Revio tumour/normal pairs this is 1.5--5.1 Gbp of contigs in place of 185--202 Gbp of tumour reads,
a 40--124$\times$ reduction. The same core representation selects the normal reads that decide
whether a candidate is germline, 1.56\% of the normal read set on HCC1954, which answers that
question as well as the whole set does. On the CASTLE panel, scored with \Minda{} against the
4-caller ensemble truth, \Snape{} reaches an F1 of 0.870 on HCC1954 against 0.871 for the published
\Severus{} calls, and 0.750--0.852 on the remaining three pairs; on simulated whole-genome tumours it
recovers 526 of 600 junctions at 99.7\% precision. HCC1954 is processed end to end, from two read
sets to a VCF, in 47 minutes on 16 cores, against 4 hours 7 minutes for the same pair through an
aligner and \Severus{} on the same machine.

\section{Background}

Structural variants drive cancer genome evolution, and long reads resolve them where short reads
cannot: a single PacBio HiFi or Oxford Nanopore read spans a breakpoint, its repetitive flanks and
often the whole event~\cite{severus,savana}. The methods built on this share one design. Both the
tumour and the matched normal read set are aligned to a reference genome; breakpoints are then read
off the alignments as split reads and long CIGAR indels, clustered, and finally subtracted between
the two samples, so that what remains is somatic. \Severus{} does this on a phased breakpoint
graph~\cite{severus}, \textsc{Savana} with a random forest over alignment features~\cite{savana},
and \textsc{nanomonsv} by assembling supporting reads around each candidate
junction~\cite{nanomonsv}.

Two properties follow from that order of operations. First, the alignment of both samples is the
dominant cost: a 30$\times$ human tumour/normal pair is roughly 200 Gbp of sequence, and the pairs
used here are 391 Gbp; every base of it is placed against the reference before the first somatic
decision is taken. Second, the
aligner decides the coordinates of sequence that a somatic event has, by definition, moved. A
junction joins two loci that are not adjacent in the reference; a tumour haplotype may carry a copy
of a sequence that the reference holds once, or holds nowhere.

The somatic question itself does not require a reference. It is a comparison between two read sets:
which sequence do the tumour reads carry that the normal reads do not? What that comparison needs is
a way to recognise the same locus in two different reads, and Locally Consistent Parsing (LCP)
provides exactly that. LCP partitions a string into substrings called \emph{cores} under two
guarantees~\cite{sv94a,sv94b}: \emph{partition consistency}, so that identical content is partitioned
identically wherever it occurs, and \emph{labeling consistency}, so that cores over the same
sequence receive the same label. Applied iteratively with Deterministic Coin Tossing~\cite{dct},
each level reduces the number of cores by a constant factor of $c \approx 2.34$ while lengthening
them by the same factor~\cite{lcpan}. \Lcptools{} makes this practical for DNA~\cite{lcpan}, and two
applications have followed: \Lcpan{} builds variation graphs an order of magnitude faster and with
an order of magnitude less memory than \textsc{vg}~\cite{lcpan}, and \Gencore{} estimates genomic
distances between closely related large genomes directly from cores, including from unassembled HiFi
reads~\cite{gencore}. Unlike sketching, LCP subsamples nothing: every base of the input is covered
by at least one core, which is what allows two read sets to be compared without deciding in advance
which parts of them matter.

Here we present \Snape, which uses LCP cores to make the somatic decision before any alignment. Both
read sets are parsed and counted into a single two-coloured core graph, where colour is the pair of
per-sample read counts on a core; the somatic material is the set of cores the tumour reads produce
and the normal reads never do. \Snape{} then carves that material out of the reads that carry it as
short \emph{junction contigs} --- a somatic run with a germline anchor on each side --- and hands
only those to an aligner, which places them and nothing else. The germline status of each surviving
candidate is settled by the normal reads at its locus, and the same core representation names those
reads: they are the normal reads that share cores with the candidate's own germline anchors, 1.56\%
of the normal read set. The reference is therefore consulted once, for 1--3\% of the input, and only
after the somatic decision has been made.

\section{Results}

We evaluated \Snape{} on four PacBio Revio tumour/normal cell-line pairs from the CASTLE panel and on
simulated whole-genome tumours. All measurements were made on a 16-core AMD Ryzen 9950X workstation
with 157 GB of memory, with every stage pinned to the same 16 cores.

\subsection{Data and evaluation}

The four pairs (Table~\ref{tab:data}) are the HiFi arm of the CASTLE panel (Cancer Standards
Long-read Evaluation), BioProject PRJNA1086849. Each tumour read set is 185--202 Gbp, about
60--65$\times$ of the human genome, and the matched normals are of the same order. Their truth
sets are the panel's \texttt{minda\_4\_2} ensembles: a somatic SV enters the truth when at least two
callers in each of three platform groups, and at least four callers overall, report it. All accuracy
numbers below come from \Minda~\cite{minda} at the settings the panel uses, a 500 bp breakpoint
tolerance and a 50 bp size floor, and \Severus{}~\cite{severus} is scored from its published calls
on the same pairs with the same command, so the comparison is symmetric.

\subsection{The somatic material of a tumour is a small part of its core graph}

Parsing both read sets at level 5 and counting the cores yields a graph of $7.0 \times 10^{7}$
nodes and $7.5 \times 10^{7}$ edges for every pair (Table~\ref{tab:graph}), 2.3 GB on disk, in which
a node is a 64-bit core label and its two read counts. The proportions of that graph are the first
result. Cores carried only by the tumour reads --- the somatic hypothesis, before any filtering ---
are 0.54--1.48\% of the nodes; cores carried only by the normal reads are 2.35--9.04\%. Somatic
material is thus roughly one percent of the graph, and it is available for inspection without a
reference, an assembly or an alignment.

Most of that one percent is not a structural variant. A somatic single-nucleotide variant, or an
indel below the reporting floor, also produces tumour-only cores, and each such core would otherwise
become a candidate and a contig to align. These are separated from junctions by the shape of the
bubble they form: a local edit leaves the two arms of the bubble the same length and colinear, while
a novel adjacency does not (Section~\ref{sec:bubbles}). That test removes between 48.6 and 63.5 per
cent of the somatic cores across the four pairs.

\subsection{Junction contigs replace the read set at the aligner}

A chunk is a connected component of the somatic subgraph: one junction candidate, identified with no
coordinates. For each chunk, \Snape{} takes the first five tumour reads that carry it and cuts from
each the span running from 50 cores before the somatic run to 50 cores after it --- the novel
sequence with about 5 kb of germline anchor on either side (Fig.~\ref{fig:contig}). No assembly and
no cross-read merging is needed to make that cut: consecutive cores of one read are adjacent and
overlapping in that read's own bytes, so the span between two core boundaries is the sequence
itself (Algorithm~\ref{alg:contigs}).

What reaches the aligner is therefore 165,314--549,450 contigs of a mean 9.0--9.2 kb
(Table~\ref{tab:graph}), which is 1.49--5.08 Gbp against the 185--202 Gbp of tumour reads they were
cut from: a reduction of 40--124$\times$ in the sequence to be placed. The reduction tracks the
somatic content of each tumour, being largest for HCC1937 and smallest for the SV-rich H2009.

\subsection{The normal reads that answer the germline question}

The graph decides somatic by exact core sequence, and inside a repeat that is not the same question
as somatic: the tumour and normal reads may not spell one locus with the same 217 bp core --- a
different number of units in a tandem array, a different paralogous copy sampled --- while the
variant itself is shared. What settles those cases is the normal reads at the locus, in reference
space, and it settles a large number of them. On HCC1954 the contigs alone produce 1,601 calls, at a
precision of 0.587 and a recall of 0.849; asking the normal at each of their loci removes 554, of
which 552 are false and 2 are true, for a precision of 0.895 at a recall of 0.847. It is the single largest
contribution to precision in the pipeline.

Aligning the whole normal read set to ask that question would restore the cost the method exists to
avoid. \Snape{} instead asks the graph which normal reads cover the candidate loci. Each candidate
names the contig it came from; that contig's germline anchors are sequence the normal has as well,
and a normal read carrying the locus shares cores with them. Selecting every normal read that shares
at least five anchor cores yields 176,912 of 11,337,735 reads on HCC1954, 1.56\% of the sample
(Table~\ref{tab:pick}): 1,735 candidate contigs contribute 119,338 anchor labels, and one pass over
the normal read set selects the reads that carry them in five minutes. Aligning that selection
instead of the whole read set changes precision by 0.004 and recall not at all, and replaces hours
of alignment with eleven and a half minutes: minimap2 places these reads at 19.1 Mbp/s on this
machine (6.0 M reads, 97 Gbp, in 5,087 s at 19.1 GB peak), so the whole 195 Gbp normal is a
multi-hour job on its own.

\subsection{Accuracy on the CASTLE panel}

Table~\ref{tab:acc} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pr} give precision, recall and F1 for the four pairs against
the 4-caller ensemble truth, with the published \Severus{} calls scored identically. On HCC1954
\Snape{} reaches an F1 of 0.870 against 0.871 --- ahead on recall by 0.021, behind on precision by
0.026, and 0.001 short overall. On the other three pairs it reaches 0.750--0.852 against
0.810--0.903.

The comparison carries a measurable asymmetry: \Severus{} is one of the callers whose output built
the ensemble, so its calls are in that truth partly by construction. The panel publishes two looser
ensembles for HCC1954, and scoring one and the same pair of call sets against all three
(Table~\ref{tab:ens}) puts a size on it. Of the 110 \Snape{} calls the 4-caller ensemble counts false,
47 are events the 2-caller ensemble contains. As the truth widens, both callers' precision rises and
both recalls fall, and \Snape{} is ahead on F1 on the two wider ensembles.

\subsection{Simulated whole-genome tumours}

The development benchmark is a simulated pair with an exact truth: a tumour genome carrying 600
structural variants, 118,650 single-nucleotide variants and 6,580 short indels is built from the
reference with insilicoSV~\cite{insilicosv}, and 30$\times$ HiFi reads are simulated from it and from
the unmodified reference with Badread~\cite{badread}. Because the variants are written into the
genome before sequencing, the truth is the set of novel adjacencies the tumour genome contains, and
scoring is done at the junction level: a deletion contributes one junction, an inversion two, and a
complex event as many as its grammar creates. At its shipped defaults \Snape{} recovers 526 of the 600 junctions and 70 of
the 78 insertions, at a precision of 99.7\%: two of its 593 calls do not match a truth junction.
Recall is 91.0\% for deletions, 92.3\% for inversions and 76.9\% for duplications, the last being
where a tandem array of the duplicated unit leaves the fewest cores to work with.

\section{Discussion}

\Snape{} shows that the somatic decision in a tumour/normal pair can be made in core space, before a
reference is consulted, and that doing so leaves an alignment problem one to two orders of magnitude
smaller than the original one. The two properties of LCP that carry the method are the ones its
theory guarantees: partition consistency makes a locus produce the same core in a tumour read and in
a normal read, so the two samples can be compared by label; and the fact that consecutive cores
overlap makes a somatic region excisable from a single read, which is what turns a set of tumour-only
labels back into sequence an aligner can place.

Level 5 is what these results are measured at. It puts a core at a median of 217 bp of raw read,
which is long enough that a core is specific and short enough that a somatic event perturbs only a
handful of them; the same choice makes a core insensitive to a 50 bp change, which is why the bubble
test that separates local edits from junctions is carried out on minimizers of the arms rather than
on core labels. A systematic study of level selection against event size is future work, and the
level-to-length relation measured in~\cite{lcpan} makes it a well-posed one.

The recall gap on HCC1937 is upstream of the caller: for 66\% of the missed events, no contig reaches
either breakpoint, so the material is absent before any threshold is applied. Two mechanisms are
available for that. Chunks that are carried by reads but never with enough anchor on both sides ---
2,705 of 110,561 on HCC1954 --- are exactly the chunks a cross-read merge in core space would
recover, and merging is well defined here because adjacent cores overlap. Second, an adjacency can be
tumour-only while both of its endpoint cores are germline, in which case the junction leaves no
somatic core at all; those adjacencies are already present in the graph as edges with a tumour count
and no normal count, and using them as chunk seeds is a direct extension of the current chunk
definition.

Finally, the two-coloured core graph carries more than the caller currently reads. A chunk's tumour
support relative to the coverage of the germline cores it hangs off is a local allele fraction, and
it is available before any alignment; the edges carry per-colour traversal counts of their own, which
no stage reads today. The same construction also extends past two samples: a multi-region or
longitudinal series enters as one graph with one count vector per node, in which the shared and the
private material of each sample are read off in exactly the same way. We leave both to future work.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Cores and their labels}

\Snape{} parses every read with \Lcptools~\cite{lcpan} at level $\ell = 5$. Write $\Phi_\ell(s)$ for
the sequence of cores the parse produces on a string $s$. The two guarantees the method rests
on~\cite{sv94a,sv94b} are, in the form stated in~\cite{lcpan}:

\begin{definition}[Partition consistency]
Given an input string $S$ and two occurrences $X_i$, $X_j$ of the same substring, $X_i$ and $X_j$
are partitioned in the same way, with the possible exception of their left and right margins.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Labeling consistency]
Cores that consist of the same characters are assigned the same label.
\end{definition}

A read $r$ is represented by the labels of its cores. Two reads covering the same locus therefore
emit the same labels over it, whichever sample they come from, and this --- not alignment --- is what
lets \Snape{} equate sequence across reads.

Two adjustments make the representation robust to the errors the data actually carries. First, cores
within a margin $\mu = 10$ bp of either read end are discarded: partition consistency is a statement
about interior content, and a core cut by a read boundary is not reproducible. Second, the label is
computed over the \emph{homopolymer-compressed} bytes of the core: runs of one repeated base are
collapsed to a single base before the parse, with \textsc{MurmurHash64A}~\cite{murmur} as the hash, so
that a run-length slip does not change a locus's identity. Core start and end positions are mapped
back to raw read coordinates, so nothing downstream reasons in compressed space. A level-5 core is
therefore about 150 parsed bases~\cite{lcpan}, which on human sequence is a median of 217 bp of raw
read.

The compression is calibrated on the reads themselves rather than assumed. Comparing HCC1954's normal
reads to each other over 5 Mb of chromosome 1 (11,824 reads) and calling a difference an error when
at most 20\% of the reads covering that position carry it, 83.4\% of errors are indels (49.2\%
deletion, 34.3\% insertion) and 16.6\% are substitutions. The indel errors are enriched
2.3$\times$ in homopolymer runs of length $\geq 3$ relative to the composition of the same window,
and depleted 0.4$\times$ outside runs, while substitution errors --- the control --- show no
enrichment at all (1.0--1.1$\times$). Eighty percent of indel errors fall inside a run of length
$\geq 2$ and are neutralised by the compression.

\subsection{The two-coloured core graph}

Let $\mathcal{R}_N$ and $\mathcal{R}_T$ be the normal and tumour read sets. Because the parse is not
equivariant under reverse complementation --- a consequence of the LMAX rule's neighbour guards,
which have no LMIN mirror --- each read is parsed on both strands and both parses contribute. For a
label $x$, let $c_N(x)$ and $c_T(x)$ be the number of retained cores carrying $x$ in each sample. The
node set is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nodes}
V = \{\,x : c_N(x) \geq \theta \ \text{ or }\ c_T(x) \geq \theta\,\},\qquad \theta = 3,
\end{equation}
a label seen fewer than $\theta$ times in both samples being read error. Colour is not stored; it is
derived from the counts,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:colour}
\chi(x) = \begin{cases}
\mathsf{T} & c_N(x) = 0 < c_T(x) \quad\text{(somatic)},\\
\mathsf{N} & c_T(x) = 0 < c_N(x),\\
\mathsf{S} & c_N(x), c_T(x) > 0 \quad\text{(shared)}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Edges are the adjacencies the reads traverse: for every pair of consecutive retained cores in a read,
$(u,v)$ is incremented in that read's colour, and an edge is kept when both of its endpoints are in
$V$. The result is a directed graph with a per-colour count on every node and every edge, holding no
coordinates and no sequence,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graph}
\mathcal{G} = \bigl(V,\ E,\ (c_N, c_T),\ (e_N, e_T)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Both node and edge counting are done in one streaming pass over the two read sets, with per-worker
hash maps merged into 64 label-sharded global maps; the node floor \eqref{eq:nodes} and the
both-endpoints-survive rule for edges are applied once, at the end, on exact counts.

What makes $\chi$ usable as a somatic decision is the pair of guarantees above, in the following
form.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:somatic}
Let a tumour haplotype join two loci whose sequences the normal also carries, with at least the LCP
window of flanking sequence on each side of the join, and let both alleles be sequenced to at least
$\theta$ reads. Then \emph{(i)} the cores strictly inside the two flanks are the cores the normal
produces there, and \emph{(ii)} a core spanning the join receives a label that no normal read
produces, unless the same adjacency, or a sequence with the same compressed bases, occurs elsewhere
in the normal.
\end{proposition}

Part (i) is partition consistency applied to the flanks, whose content is unchanged by the join;
part (ii) is labeling consistency read in the contrapositive --- a label is a function of the core's
bases, so a core over bases the normal never sequences cannot carry a label the normal produced. The
exception in (ii) is not hypothetical: in a tandem array or a segmental duplication the two samples
may spell one locus with different cores while carrying the same allele, which is why \Snape{} puts the
surviving candidates to the normal reads once more in reference space
(Section~\ref{sec:germline}).

\subsection{Separating sub-SV edits from junctions}\label{sec:bubbles}

A \emph{bubble} is a node with out-degree at least two whose two node-disjoint linear arms converge
on a common node, with exactly one arm somatic. Both cases of interest have this shape: a somatic
SNV or short indel, and a novel adjacency into sequence the normal also carries. They differ in
whether the somatic arm is the germline arm with a local change, and at level 5 the core labels
cannot see that difference --- a 217 bp core either matches or does not.

The arms are therefore compared on their sequence, through canonical minimizers ($k=21$, $w=22$,
about 13 per core) taken on the homopolymer-compressed bases, and the comparison is ordered rather
than set-based: a junction core is built from the two flanks' material, so shared minimizers alone
say nothing. Writing $m_S$ and $m_G$ for the minimizer sequences of the somatic and germline arms and
$\mathrm{lcs}$ for the length of their longest common subsequence, the somatic arm is classified as a
local edit when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bubble}
\bigl||m_S| - |m_G|\bigr| \leq 12
\quad\text{and}\quad
|m_S| - \mathrm{lcs}(m_S, m_G) \leq 20 ,
\end{equation}
that is, when the two arms are of nearly equal length and the somatic arm is almost entirely
colinear with the germline one. The labels of arms satisfying \eqref{eq:bubble} are excluded from the
somatic set before chunks are formed.

\subsection{Junction contigs}

The remaining somatic labels induce a subgraph, and each of its connected components is a
\emph{chunk}: one junction candidate. The tumour reads are then streamed once. For a read carrying a
chunk, let $i$ and $j$ be the indices of the first and last of its retained cores belonging to that
chunk; the emitted contig is the substring of that read between the start of core $i - p$ and the end
of core $j + p$, with $p = 50$ cores of padding. Somatic runs separated by fewer than $2p$ cores are
merged into one window, so that an event whose interior is reference sequence --- a mobile element
insertion, whose two joins are somatic while its body is not --- is emitted as a single contig
spanning both joins. A window whose achieved anchor is shorter than 300 bp on either side is not
emitted, and at most five contigs are kept per chunk. Contigs are named per read and per window, so
that a read carrying two junctions supports each of them once.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\SetAlgoLined
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{graph $\mathcal{G}$, tumour reads $\mathcal{R}_T$, edit labels $D$, padding $p$, cap $k$, minimum flank $f$}
\KwOut{junction contigs}
$S \gets \{x \in V : \chi(x) = \mathsf{T}\} \setminus D$\;
$\mathcal{C} \gets$ connected components of the subgraph induced by $S$\;
\ForEach{read $r \in \mathcal{R}_T$}{
  $(x_1,\dots,x_m) \gets$ the retained core labels of $r$, in read order\;
  \ForEach{maximal run $x_i,\dots,x_j$ of labels of one component $c \in \mathcal{C}$, runs less than
           $2p$ cores apart being merged}{
    \lIf{$\mathrm{taken}[c] \geq k$}{\textbf{continue}}
    $a \gets \max(1, i-p)$;\quad $b \gets \min(m, j+p)$\;
    \If{$\mathrm{start}(x_i) - \mathrm{start}(x_a) \geq f$ \textbf{and} $\mathrm{end}(x_b) - \mathrm{end}(x_j) \geq f$}{
      emit the substring of $r$ from $\mathrm{start}(x_a)$ to $\mathrm{end}(x_b)$\;
      $\mathrm{taken}[c] \gets \mathrm{taken}[c] + 1$\;
    }
  }
}
\caption{Junction contigs from the two-coloured core graph. Defaults: $p = 50$ cores, $k = 5$ contigs
per component, $f = 300$ bp.}
\label{alg:contigs}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Breakpoints from the aligned contigs}

Contigs are aligned with \texttt{minimap2 -ax map-hifi -Y -{}-secondary=no}~\cite{minimap2} and sorted
with \texttt{samtools}~\cite{samtools}. \Snape{} then reads the alignments and reports a junction as a
pair of reference sides with the direction each side leaves the reference in: $d = +1$ when the
alignment ends there going right, $d = -1$ when it starts there going left. That pair is the whole
type vocabulary --- $(+,-)$ a deletion, $(-,+)$ a duplication, equal signs an inversion, two
different contigs a breakend --- so no separate typing step is required. Junctions come from
consecutive alignments of one contig chained in \emph{query} order, and from CIGAR indels at or above
the 50 bp floor.

Three gates act on the alignments. An alignment whose substitution rate exceeds 1\% is discarded:
with reads at 0.018\% error, such an alignment is not the locus the contig came from. Inserted and
deleted bases are excluded from that rate, since a contig carrying a real 400 bp deletion would
otherwise score as if it were misplaced. Second, a short tandem duplication is not emitted as a split
by the aligner but as an insertion; the duplicated copy is present in the contig on one side or the
other of the inserted bases, and comparing the two, which needs no reference, recovers the duplicated
interval. Third, a short inversion is not emitted as a split either --- the aligner walks through it
and pays with a dense run of small indels --- and neither is a variable-number tandem repeat. The two
are separated by the tandem-repeat content of the window's own bases, measured as the largest
fraction of positions agreeing with the position $p$ before them over $p \in \{1,\dots,6\}$, which is
$\approx 0.25$ for random sequence; a window above 0.40 is not a breakpoint, and a call whose anchor
is above 0.50 is dropped.

Junctions agreeing on both sides to within 50 bp, with the same contig pair and the same directions,
are one call, positioned at the median of its supporting breakpoints, and a call is reported when at
least five distinct contigs support it. The candidate pass that feeds Section~\ref{sec:germline} runs
the same code at a floor of four, one below the reporting floor, so that a call sitting at the
boundary still has its normal reads aligned: a candidate locus too many costs alignment, never
precision.

\subsection{The germline question, and the normal reads that answer it}
\label{sec:germline}

For each surviving call, \Snape{} reads the normal alignments at its locus and drops the call when
either the same event is present there --- an indel of comparable size within the window --- or the
normal cannot place the locus either, taken as a quarter or more of the normal reads carrying more
than 2\% substitutions. A breakend is examined at both of its ends.

The normal reads to align are chosen in core space. Every call records the contig it came from; the
germline anchors of those contigs, being the flanking regions rather than the somatic middle, are
sequence the normal also carries. \Snape{} parses those anchors, collects their core labels, streams
the normal read set once, and emits every read sharing at least five labels with the collection.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Real-data accuracy is measured with \Minda~\cite{minda},
\begin{verbatim}
minda truthset --base <ensemble>.vcf --vcfs <calls>.vcf \
               --min_size 50 --tolerance 500
\end{verbatim}
which is the tool and the setting the CASTLE panel reports against, applied unchanged to the
\Severus{} calls published with the panel and to ours. On the simulation, where the truth is the set
of novel adjacencies insilicoSV wrote into the tumour genome, calls are scored at the junction level
with a 100 bp tolerance: a call matches when both of its breakpoints fall within the tolerance of a
truth adjacency's two breakpoints, on the right contigs, in either pairing order. Insertions have no
adjacency of their own and are matched on position and length.

\subsection{Implementation}

\Snape{} is implemented in C++17 as a single binary of about 2,300 lines over \Lcptools~\cite{lcpan},
with \texttt{htslib}~\cite{samtools} for alignment input. Read sets are accepted as uncompressed
FASTQ or as a 2-bit packed format the binary reads and writes, and every stage takes either. Each
stage writes one checkpoint, and a run resumes from the last one present.
